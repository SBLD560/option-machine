02Position
Dear OMA,

Your process starts by testing many options and ends with one sharp decision. This quarter, AI made the options cheap for us: façade variants, renders and massing studies we could never have drawn by hand in the time.

It never made the decision. Every useful output went through our judgment, and every failure was caught by someone who knew how to draw.

This pamphlet proposes where AI belongs at OMA: in competitions, AMO research and media. It also says where it doesn't belong, what it costs, and what the rules should be.

Nuor Alsekooty & Tryna Okumu

07Overview
Toolkit · Ethics debate

Five motions

KEEP The class argued five motions. Our policy answers each of them.

Bias is a dataset problem that curation can correct.We trained our own LoRAs, and they inherited the bias of the images we chose. Curation moves the bias; it doesn't remove it.
Work is authored by whoever directed it.Only partly. Prompts read as instructions, not authorship. Selection, arrangement and edits by hand are what we can defend.
08Toolkit
The energy cost is justified by the output.Only when the output is used. Generating 40 options and keeping one has to be counted, not ignored.
AI gives design power to people who were locked out.True for clients and the public at workshops; less true inside a firm, where it concentrates power in whoever runs the tools.
AI frees juniors from work that never taught them much.We disagree. Drawing the section is how we caught the agent's errors.

The full debate, in short

We started between two positions. Molly Crabapple: art is human, and no one's work should be used without permission or credit. Wolf D. Prix: AI is a coworker whose mistakes open new forms, while the architect decides.

The creative-control reading settled it: a prompt is an instruction, not authorship. What we can defend is selection, arrangement and hand edits, so every policy rule keeps a person deciding.

Toolkit · Custom LoRA

Teaching it our eye

Flux.2 Klein 9B editCustom LoRA · fal.aiCompetitionMarketing

We trained edit-LoRAs (Colorful, Realistic and MIR-style) on paired images: an untextured Rhino render as the start image and a finished image as the end. The MIR set kept 20 pairs; rejected pairs went to a separate dropped folder.

Win

The most efficient way to render: one consistent house style across every view, without changing the design details of our model.

Loss

The LoRA is only as good as its training pairs. We had to curate and drop pairs by hand, and the right strength changes from image to image.

KEEP Our house style on every render, at a strength picked by eye.

15Toolkit
Toolkit · Custom LoRA · losses

What the LoRA got wrong

We ran the same Rhino model through our Colorful LoRA at nine strengths. Drag the slider to see what each one does.

  • Too weak (0.0–1.0): the style barely moves. Every frame looks like the base model, so the LoRA adds little.
  • Too strong (1.5–1.7): the style finally shows, but the building breaks. The A-frame turns into coloured patches and the water becomes a mosaic.
  • The lesson: there was no single "right" strength. Someone has to pick it for each image, by eye.
  • Cost: each 1,000-step training run is about $6.40 at fal's published rate, so three LoRAs cost about $20 before a single render.

Toolkit · Agentic modeling · losses

What it got wrong

  • It renamed the Perspective viewport, so later view commands failed.
  • It ran grid-A columns up to the roof, although L3 starts further east.
  • It hung the façade mesh past the edge of the building.
  • It placed section trees over the balconies and a figure inside the building.
  • The bridge timed out on saves; we confirmed each one from file times.

We caught every one by looking at a drawing, not by reading the agent's report.

25Toolkit
Toolkit · Redline and revision

Redlines for a machine

We marked up the drawings in red and gave them back to the agent.

Where did the agent misread us?

It didn't understand quick redlines. A fast circle or arrow wasn't enough, so we had to send several rounds of changes before it got the revision right.

What did we fix by hand?

Furniture placement. The agent kept putting furniture in the wrong place, so we placed it in the correct location ourselves and kept sending redlines until the plan read right.

Would we do it again?

KEEP Yes. It keeps the architect in charge: we mark up, the agent revises, we check. It is the safeguard that makes agentic modeling usable.

28–29Integration
Integration · rollout

Pilot, three months, one year

Pilot · one competition

One team and one office (Rotterdam, proposed). Image to image + LoRA for option renders; the agent for massing checks. Every prompt is logged the way we did it.

30Integration

Three months

AMO writes the policy from the pilot log. An office LoRA is trained on OMA's own approved images. Two workshops per office.

One year

All four offices. Local GPUs where client data rules require them. Yearly review: what we keep, what we drop, the energy used.

How we measure the pilot

  • Hours per option set: by hand vs. with AI, logged on every competition board.
  • Errors caught in review: how many agent mistakes a drawing check finds per round.
  • Redline rounds per revision: our test needed several; the pilot should need fewer.
  • Renders that kept the design: the share used without fixing geometry or materials.
  • GPU hours per competition: reported next to the number of options made.

If the pilot doesn't save time without adding errors, we stop at three months.

33Costs
Risks and policy · draft

OMA AI policy, v0.1

1 · Client data

Client files never go into a public model; where a region requires it, work runs on local GPUs. Every project lists its tools.

2 · Authorship

A partner signs every concept. AI images are labeled in reviews, and hand edits are recorded: that's where authorship lives.

3 · Bias

Office LoRAs train only on OMA's own cleared images, never found photos. Our MIR set used one; we wouldn't repeat it. AMO reviews each set.

34Policy

4 · Energy

Every competition reports its GPU hours next to the options it made. Discarded tests still count.

5 · What juniors still learn by hand

Sections, plans, physical models, and checking a drawing against a brief. That's how we caught every agent error this quarter.

6 · Equity

Tools and training are open to every office and every level, not only the computation team.
